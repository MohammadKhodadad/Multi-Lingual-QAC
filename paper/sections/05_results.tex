\section{Results}

We organize the results around the three questions the benchmark is meant to
answer: whether generated QACs are usable, whether cross-language retrieval is
harder than same-language retrieval, and whether chemistry-aware hard negatives
surface failures that ordinary relevance metrics hide.

\paragraph{QAC quality is high enough for benchmark construction.}
Human review shows that the generated QACs are usable for benchmark
construction, with most items falling in the ``good'' bucket. Technical questions
score slightly higher than semantic questions on average (8.72 vs.\ 7.91), but
both modes remain usable for retrieval evaluation. The LLM verifier is slightly
stricter than the human annotator, supporting its use for filtering and auditing
while still requiring human validation for benchmark-quality claims.

\paragraph{Average recall hides cross-language weakness.}
Figure~\ref{fig:clir-leaderboard} shows that same-language retrieval is
consistently easier than cross-language retrieval. A model can therefore look
acceptable under an aggregate score while failing on the deployment-relevant case
where the evidence is available only in another language. The directed
language-pair view further shows that this difficulty is not symmetric: query
language and document language both matter.

\paragraph{The errors are not only multilingual; they are chemical.}
The alias-graph benchmark shows a second failure mode. Even when a retrieved
document is topically close, it may be chemically wrong. Figure~\ref{fig:alias-confusion}
measures how often a chemically confusable document outranks all gold evidence.
These failures are especially important for chemistry search because a nearby
compound, parent class, or sibling concept can look plausible while answering the
wrong question.

Together, the results support the central lesson of the benchmark: multilingual
chemistry retrieval should be evaluated with language position and chemical
confusability visible, not only with a single averaged retrieval score.
