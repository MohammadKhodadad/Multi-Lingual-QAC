\section{Results}

We organize the results around the three questions the benchmark is meant to
answer: whether generated QACs are usable, whether cross-language retrieval is
harder than same-language retrieval, and whether chemistry-aware hard negatives
surface failures that ordinary relevance metrics hide.

\paragraph{QAC quality is high enough for benchmark construction.}
Human review of 97 generated QACs gives a mean score of 8.33/10, with 94 items
falling in the ``good'' bucket. Technical questions score slightly higher than
semantic questions on average (8.72 vs.\ 7.91), but both modes remain usable for
retrieval evaluation. The LLM verifier is slightly stricter than the human
annotator (79.0\% vs.\ 83.3\%), supporting its use for filtering and auditing
while still requiring human validation for benchmark-quality claims.

\begin{figure*}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{claimA_A3_gap_heatmap.png}
    \caption{Semantic-minus-technical Recall@10 gap by model and language.
    Nearly every cell is positive, showing that technical chemistry questions
    are harder to retrieve than semantic questions across both patent sources.}
    \label{fig:technical-semantic-gap}
\end{figure*}

\paragraph{Average recall hides cross-language weakness.}
Figures~\ref{fig:technical-semantic-gap} and~\ref{fig:language-collapse} show
that recall depends on question type, language, and relevance position.
Technical questions are harder than semantic questions, and same-language gold
evidence is retrieved more easily than cross-language gold evidence in every
query language; in the updated Google Patents results, the same-vs-cross gap is
roughly 0.21--0.28 Recall@10.
A model can therefore look acceptable under an aggregate score while failing on
the deployment-relevant case where the evidence is available only in another
language.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{claimC_C3_home_advantage.png}
    \caption{Recall@10 separates into same-language gold retrieval and
    cross-language gold retrieval. Same-language evidence is consistently easier
    to retrieve, which explains why average recall can hide the failure most
    relevant to multilingual chemistry search.}
    \label{fig:language-collapse}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The errors are not only multilingual; they are chemical.}
The alias-graph benchmark shows a second failure mode. Even when a retrieved
document is topically close, it may be chemically wrong. Figure~\ref{fig:confusion-heatmap}
measures how often a chemically confusable document outranks all gold evidence;
in the updated alias-graph results, this occurs on 14--48\% of queries depending on the
model. These failures are especially important for chemistry search because a
nearby compound, parent class, or sibling concept can look plausible while
answering the wrong question.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{claimD_D1_confusion_heatmap.png}
    \caption{Confusion rate by model and query language: how often a chemically
    plausible but wrong look-alike outranks every gold document. Lower rates are
    better, and the spread shows why chemistry-aware hard negatives are needed.}
    \label{fig:confusion-heatmap}
\end{figure}

Together, the results support the central lesson of the benchmark: multilingual
chemistry retrieval should be evaluated with language position and chemical
confusability visible, not only with a single averaged retrieval score.
