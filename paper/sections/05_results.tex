\section{Results}

We organize the results around the three questions the benchmark is meant to
answer: whether generated QACs are usable, whether cross-language retrieval is
harder than same-language retrieval, and whether chemistry-aware hard negatives
surface failures that ordinary relevance metrics hide.

\paragraph{QAC quality is high enough for benchmark construction.}
Human review of 97 generated QACs gives a mean score of 8.33/10, with 94 of 97
items falling in the ``good'' bucket. Technical questions score slightly higher
than semantic questions on average (8.72 vs.\ 7.91), but both modes remain usable
for retrieval evaluation. Comparing normalized human and LLM-grader scores, the
LLM grader is slightly stricter than the human annotator on average (79.0\% vs.\
83.3\%), with a mean absolute difference of 8.22 percentage points. We read this
as evidence that the automatic verifier is useful for filtering and auditing,
while still requiring human validation for claims about benchmark quality.

\paragraph{Average recall hides cross-language weakness.}
The cross-lingual benchmark shows that standard Recall@10 is not enough to judge
a multilingual retriever. In Figure~\ref{fig:clir-leaderboard}, same-language
retrieval is consistently easier than cross-language retrieval: the best
cross-language Recall@10 is 0.50, and the same-language home advantage reaches
+0.55 for the most biased model. This means a model can look acceptable under an
aggregate score while failing on the deployment-relevant case where the evidence
is available only in another language. The directed language-pair view further
shows that the difficulty is not symmetric: query language and document language
both matter.

\paragraph{The errors are not only multilingual; they are chemical.}
The alias-graph benchmark shows a second failure mode. Even when a retrieved
document is topically close, it may be chemically wrong. Figure~\ref{fig:alias-confusion}
measures how often a chemically confusable document outranks all gold evidence;
on the publication lens, this happens on 14--78\% of queries depending on the
model. These failures are especially important for chemistry search because a
nearby compound, parent class, or sibling concept can look plausible while
answering the wrong question.

Together, the results support the central lesson of the benchmark: multilingual
chemistry retrieval should be evaluated with language position and chemical
confusability visible, not only with a single averaged retrieval score.
