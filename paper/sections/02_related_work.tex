\section{Related Work}

General embedding benchmarks such as MTEB and MMTEB, together with multilingual
and cross-lingual retrieval resources such as MIRACL, NeuCLIR, and CLIRMatrix,
have enabled systematic comparison of retrieval and embedding models across
languages
\citep{miracl2023,neuclir2023,muennighoff2023mteb,mmteb2025,clirmatrix2020}.
These resources establish the broader evaluation setting, but they do not provide
retrieval data designed specifically for multilingual chemistry search. In
particular, they do not combine chemistry-specific relevance, controlled
multilingual patent variants, explicit query- and document-language metadata, and
chemically plausible hard negatives. Our benchmark suite builds on this line of
work while adding the domain structure needed to audit multilingual retrieval in
technical chemistry settings.

Chemistry-specific benchmarks have recently expanded evaluation beyond generic
NLP tasks. ChemTEB evaluates chemical text embeddings across retrieval,
classification, clustering, bitext mining, and pair-classification tasks
\citep{chemteb2024}; ChemLit-QA provides expert-validated QAC data for chemistry
RAG \citep{chemlitqa2024}; and ChemComp and ChemKGMultiHopQA test
chemistry-focused multi-hop scientific reasoning in retrieval-aware settings
\citep{khodadad2026chemcomp,astaraki2026iterativerag}. Recent work on chemical
literature embeddings, such as ChEmbed, further demonstrates the value of
domain-adapted retrieval models and chemistry-specific evaluation
\citep{chembed2025}. Together, these resources establish the need for
domain-specific chemistry evaluation, but they do not provide a multilingual
chemistry retrieval benchmark that combines controlled multilingual patent
variants, explicit same-language and cross-language relevance judgments, and
chemically plausible hard negatives. Our work complements these efforts by
focusing on multilingual retrieval robustness rather than broad chemistry QA,
multi-hop reasoning, or general-purpose chemical embedding evaluation.

LLM-assisted benchmark construction can reduce the cost of producing
domain-specific evaluation data, but generated items require grounding,
provenance, automated filtering, and human audit before they can support reliable
conclusions. Our pipeline applies this idea to retrieval benchmark construction
by preserving source-document and language metadata and exporting each selected
QAC with scorer outputs and relevance artifacts.

Recent work on cross-lingual retrieval and multilingual RAG shows that retrieval
behavior can depend strongly on language alignment, translation effects, and
same-language preference
\citep{whatdrivesclir2025,crosslingualcost2025,bordirlines2024,xrag2025,nepotism2025}.
Consequently, a model can perform well under aggregate recall while its score is
supported disproportionately by easier same-language matches. We therefore
evaluate same-language and cross-language relevance separately, including
``no-home'' cases in which no same-language gold document is available. Patent
publication variants make this comparison practical by providing multilingual
technical evidence under tighter content control than independently collected
documents across languages.

Patent retrieval has a long evaluation history, including NTCIR and CLEF-IP
\citep{prime2002,clefip2013}, and recent work has revisited patent representation
learning and embedding evaluation \citep{paecter2024,patentembeddings2026}. We
build on this history but use patents as a source of controlled multilingual
technical evidence rather than treating prior-art search as the sole target task.
To make the evaluation chemistry-aware, we use chemical ontology and alias
structure, including ChEBI \citep{chebi2016}, to construct hard negatives that
are plausible in chemistry search but wrong for the intended evidence need. This
auxiliary alias-graph stress test is complementary to ontology-grounded
extraction work such as CEAR \citep{cear2024}: rather than extracting chemical
entities and roles, it tests whether a retriever can distinguish the intended
evidence from chemically related but incorrect documents.
